The rain began before dawn, not as weather but as a warning. It slid down the copper roofs of Veyr Station in long green threads, gathered in the gutters, and carried fragments of light from the advertisements that never switched off. By the time Mara Vale reached the tram vaults, every window in the district showed a different version of the city.

Veyr had once been built around a library so large that no map could contain it. People called it the Glass Archive because its towers were transparent and because nothing placed inside it was supposed to be forgotten. That promise had collapsed seventeen years earlier, when the Archive locked its doors, erased its catalogs, and began answering questions no one had asked.

"Do not touch the blue seals," Mara said, stopping under the broken clock at the station entrance. "If the Archive still recognizes my authorization, it will open one layer at a time. If it does not, the seals will decide we are vandalism."

"I like how you make death sound administrative," Idris replied. "It gives the whole expedition a professional tone."

"Professional would mean bringing a second battery, a rope, and a reason to trust either of you," Sera said. "We have one battery, half a rope, and no reason."

They had met only three nights earlier in the burned market beneath the east viaduct. Mara had been looking for a ledger that should not exist. Idris had been selling illegal street maps that rearranged themselves when police drones passed overhead. Sera had been dismantling a surveillance pylon with the efficient irritation of someone correcting a badly written equation.

Chapter 1: The tram vaults. The nearer they came to the sealed heart of the Glass Archive, the more the building behaved like a witness correcting testimony. It did not merely remember events. It arranged them, weighed them, and punished anyone who mistook arrangement for innocence.

The corridor opened into a room wider than the tram vaults beneath the city. Oil, wet stone, and overheated wire moved through the room like an animal looking for a name.

At each threshold, the building asked a different question without using words. Who owns a memory after the witness dies. Whether a map is a promise or a threat. Whether a machine that preserves truth can become a liar simply by choosing the order in which truths appear.

Behind them, the route they had taken changed. A staircase became a wall of index cards; a door became a portrait; a drain became a small black window with rain falling upward inside it. The Archive was not trapping them exactly. It was editing their exit.

Mara kept one hand near the old authorization badge in her coat. Idris walked as if every tile might invoice him for trespass. Sera counted pulses from the walls under her breath, translating architecture into timing, timing into weakness, and weakness into a plan.

"The signal is recursive," Sera said. "It is not detecting us. It is detecting its own prediction of us, then punishing the difference."

"There. A maintenance hatch behind the saint with the cracked eye."

"Less art criticism. More hatch."

Wisdom was too expensive, certainty too theatrical, and innocence unavailable in a building designed to invoice the past.
